\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) affects roughly one in five people with diabetes, an estimated
103 million adults in 2020 and a projected 161 million by 2045, and it remains a leading cause
of preventable blindness in the working-age population\cite{teo2021prevalence}. Diabetic
macular oedema (DME), its most common mechanism of central vision loss, affects about one in
fifteen\cite{yau2012prevalence}. Deep learning systems that grade fundus photographs reached
specialist-level agreement in development studies a decade ago\cite{gulshan2016,ting2017}, and
autonomous screening devices have since been authorised on the strength of prospective pivotal
trials\cite{abramoff2018pivotal,ipp2021eyeart}. Yet the same class of system behaves very
differently once it leaves the population it was built on. In a head-to-head evaluation of
seven commercial algorithms on the same real-world images, sensitivity for referable disease
ranged from 51\% to 86\%\cite{lee2021headtohead}; three commercial algorithms applied to the
same eyes against the same reference standard in Indian public-health clinics returned
specificities between 14\% and 96\%\cite{duggal2025realworld}.

When a model fails on a new population, the failure is usually read as a statement about what
the network has learned. A recent frozen-encoder benchmark found that two of three encoders,
including the retinal foundation model RETFound, did not identify a single one of 270 mild
cases in an external corpus (grade-1 F1 = 0.000), and attributed the collapse to the
encoders' representations\cite{poyrazer2026calibration}. RETFound itself was shown to be
superior using macro-averaged, threshold-free metrics\cite{zhou2023retfound}, which cannot
reveal a per-grade failure of this kind. Comparisons between architectures, heads and
pretraining schemes are reported in the same way: as differences in what a model represents.

An ordinal grading model does not output a grade, however. It outputs scores that a decision
rule turns into a grade, and the rule is almost never examined. Three different things are
routinely conflated: \emph{probability calibration}, whether predicted probabilities match
observed frequencies\cite{guo2017calibration,vancalster2019calibration}; the
\emph{decision rule}, the cut-points or referral threshold that map scores to a grade or a
referral; and the \emph{representation}, what the encoder has learned. The common default,
a threshold of 0.5 on every ordinal output or an argmax, is a hyper-parameter that has been
chosen implicitly rather than one that has been avoided. If a model ranks images correctly but
its cut-points sit in the wrong place for a new population, it will look blind to a grade it
in fact separates, and two models that differ mainly in where their defaults happen to fall
will look as if they differ in what they learned. Clinical prediction research has long
treated recalibration and model updating as a routine step before
deployment\cite{janssen2008updating,vergouwe2017updating}; DR grading studies rarely test
whether their headline failures and gains survive it.

Here we test that alternative directly. We trained and archived 13 grading configurations on a
pooled development set of 2,260 images, evaluated them on three external directions
(\pn{n.aptos} APTOS images, \pn{n.ddr} DDR images from 147 Chinese hospitals, and the reverse
direction, APTOS to Messidor-2 and IDRiD), and asked, for every reported failure and every
reported difference, whether it survives \emph{matched calibration}: refitting the decision
rule of every model by the same procedure before comparing them. Every training run and every
hypothesis was pre-registered with its falsifying outcome before launch\cite{nosek2018prereg},
and we report the interventions that failed with the same weight as those that
worked.

We find that the decision rule, not the representation, explains the external collapse of the
mild grade and most apparent differences between models. Moving cut-points alone, with no
retraining, raised external mild-DR recall several-fold; the choice among deployable decision
rules changed clinically decisive metrics more than the choice among models; quadratic
weighted kappa (QWK), the field's standard summary, was nearly flat across operating points
that differed by tens of points in referral sensitivity; and models that differed only by
random seed produced per-grade ``effects'' of the size that the literature attributes to
architecture. Operating points fitted on development data did not transfer to new
populations, while about 200 locally labelled images were enough to recalibrate safely. By
contrast, eight of nine pre-registered interventions on the representation itself were
falsified.
