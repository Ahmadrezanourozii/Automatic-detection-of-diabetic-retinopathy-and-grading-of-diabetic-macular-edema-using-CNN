\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The central finding is that an external failure of an ordinal DR grading model has two
separable components, and that published evaluations rarely separate them. The ranking of images
by severity transferred well to both new populations; the mapping from score to grade did not,
and its error had opposite signs on two populations, so that the same development threshold
over-referred on one and missed a third of referable disease on the other. Where ranking
survived, the loss was recoverable by moving cut-points alone: the mild grade on APTOS, which the
literature reads as representational blindness to microaneurysms, and referral sensitivity in
the reverse direction. Where ranking degraded, as for the foundation model on DDR, no
recalibration helped. The analysis is also bounded by what it falsified. The choice among
decision rules that can be deployed without external labels did not outweigh the choice of
model, and the mild-grade collapse was specific to APTOS. A reader who sees an external failure
of a grading model should therefore ask first whether the model still ranks the new population,
then where its cut-points were set and on which population, and only then what it has learned.

This reframes several recent disagreements. Frozen-encoder benchmarks report that retinal
foundation models fail on the mild grade externally\cite{poyrazer2026calibration}, while the
foundation model's own evaluation, by macro-averaged area under the curve, could not have
detected such a failure\cite{zhou2023retfound}; our foundation-model results show the same
dissociation from the other side, a better frozen representation that did not yield a better
fine-tuned grader on 2,260 images. Calibration in the probabilistic sense is not the lever
here: temperature scaling barely moves external calibration under shift\cite{poyrazer2026calibration},
and in our data the referral probability was well ranked (external area under the curve
\pn{aptos.sel.auroc} on APTOS) while the cut-points sat in the wrong place. Clinical evaluations
show the same signature at the level of deployed systems. Commercial algorithms applied to the
same eyes differed by more than 80 points in specificity\cite{duggal2025realworld}, and seven
systems on the same images spanned 35 points of sensitivity at similar negative predictive
values\cite{lee2021headtohead}. Both are consistent with operating-point variation being reported
as model variation, and neither design could separate the two, because each system was
evaluated only at its vendor's fixed operating point.

The reverse direction sharpens the point. Trained on APTOS, the same recipe did not lose the
mild grade on Messidor-2, contrary to our own pre-registered prediction; it under-graded
instead, and the cost fell on referral: at the shipped rule it referred \pn{rev.m2.R1.sens}\% of
referable eyes while ranking them with an area under the curve of \pn{rev.m2.auroc}, and
refitted cut-points restored \pn{rev.m2.R4.sens}\%. Which grade a population shift damages
therefore depends on the population, and the development models under-graded DDR in the same
way; in each case the damage lay in where the scores were cut rather than in how the images
were ranked. A system validated as safe on
its own population can miss more than half of referable disease on another without any change
in its discriminative ability, and nothing in an agreement statistic would show it.

The metric invariance we observed explains why the problem persists. QWK charges little for a
one-grade error, so a model that sends every mild case to moderate, or refers 99\% rather than
88\% of referable patients, scores almost the same. The same insensitivity has been observed in
a deployed system, where agreement with the reference standard moved from $\kappa$=0.65 to
0.72 while sensitivity and specificity each moved by about 31 points between phases, although
that change also involved retraining and a new cohort\cite{duggal2025realworld}. Metric
choice is a known source of misleading validation in image analysis\cite{reinke2024pitfalls,maierhein2024metrics};
for screening, the implication is concrete: an agreement statistic cannot stand in for the
referral operating point, and the two must be reported together.

The practical recommendations follow directly and cost no additional training. Model
comparisons should be reported at matched calibration, with the fitting procedure named,
because a per-grade verdict can depend on it (Supplementary Note~3). External evaluations
should report the operating curve and the transfer gap of any development-fitted threshold
rather than a single sensitivity, since the same threshold landed \pn{aptos.tr.e08.A.gap} points
from its target on one population and \pn{ddr.tr.e08.A.gap} on another. Deployment should include local recalibration of the
cut-points on about 200 labelled images, a procedure whose probability of harm, not only its mean
benefit, we report, in line with long-standing advice on updating prediction models for new
settings\cite{janssen2008updating,vergouwe2017updating} and with the sample sizes recommended
for external validation\cite{riley2021samplesize}. The recalibration need not change the network:
it is a re-estimation of four numbers.

The limits of the argument matter as much as its reach. First, decision rules do not explain
everything. In the APTOS-to-Messidor-2 direction, refitting the cut-points of a published model
raised mild recall several-fold but left mild F1 below 0.3, and in our own model trained on
APTOS external cut-points raised mild recall only to \pn{rev.m2.R4m.mild}\% (pre-registered O5
falsified), so part of the mild-grade failure in that direction is representational; and the
internal mild-grade errors of our own models were only partly recoverable. The claim is that the decision rule must be separated before the representation is
blamed, not that representation never matters. Second, the cut-points that recover the mild
grade were fitted on external labels; they bound what local recalibration can achieve and are
not themselves external validation, which is why the deployable rules and the sample-size
analysis are reported separately. Third, all data are public and retrospective. APTOS labels
come from a single grader, so part of any internal-to-external difference is label noise;
none of the development or external corpora publishes patient identifiers, so resampling is by
image and fellow-eye pairs may be correlated across folds; EyePACS, used for pretraining, also
contributes to the retinal foundation model's pretraining corpus\cite{zhou2023retfound}; and the
checkpoint epoch was selected on the held-out fold, which makes development estimates slightly
optimistic (median \pn{optimism.median} QWK) without affecting external ones. Fourth, the
macular-oedema head has no external validation: the one candidate corpus overlapped the
development pool by 88\%, and the three-grade label could not be derived from published
annotations, so our DME conclusions rest on 516 images and are stated as a supervision ceiling
rather than a performance claim. Finally, the reverse direction and the seed replicates use a
single architecture, and the published-model reanalysis uses one model family; the decision-rule
effect should be tested in prospective screening data, where cut-points can be fitted on an
initial local sample and then frozen.

In summary, external failures of DR grading models are routinely attributed to learned
representations, but a substantial part of them, and of the apparent differences between models,
lies in the decision rule that converts scores into grades, and that part can be diagnosed and
repaired without retraining. Reporting threshold-free discrimination beside matched-calibration
comparisons, full operating curves and the transfer gap of any threshold, and recalibrating
cut-points locally before deployment, would separate the two components and make published
results comparable with one another and with the clinical decision they are meant to support.
